What we promise

This becomes surveillance if built carelessly. These six are why people will use it rather than resent it.

🔒Your inbox screen is yours. No manager view. Ever.
🚫It never stores what messages say.
👀“Nothing needed” scores the same as a reply.
⚖️No speed ranking on individuals.
📄Never used in performance reviews.
🎚️You can turn the game off.
Leadership sees “78% of the team cleared their inbox 4+ days this week” — same information for decisions, none of the information that makes people feel watched.

Mostly built already.

A Slack bot, a web board, and one small file of data. No hard engineering — the risk is whether people like using it.

How we roll it out

Week 0
Look at the real problem

Read 30–50 genuinely ignored mentions. Usually shows a big chunk of “silence” was asks nobody could have answered.

Weeks 1–2
Two teams. No points yet.

One struggling, one keen — with a leader inside. If leaders ignore their own tasks, it dies on contact.

Week 3
Fix what annoyed people

Ask the pilot what made them roll their eyes. Remove exactly that.

Weeks 4–6
Everyone. Game on.

Points, badges and the first Friday post. Points come last on purpose — gamifying something still annoying teaches people to game it.

The WhatsApp conversation happens last of all. Only once Slack asks visibly get picked up does “keep work in Slack” sound reasonable rather than annoying.

What I need from you

  • The name. “Roo” is a placeholder — trivial to change now, awkward once people say it out loud.
  • Do we want the inbox game at all? Most fun, most sensitive. Straw-poll the pilot first.
  • Two pilot teams — one struggling, one keen, plus one leader.
  • Sign off the six promises above. Much harder to add back later than to design in.

In one line: it catches the asks flying around Slack, gives each one an owner and a date, does the chasing itself, and turns keeping on top of your messages into a two-minute daily game.
